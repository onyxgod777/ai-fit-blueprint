Rucking for Fat Loss: Why Walking With Weight Burns More and Breaks Less

October 31, 2026 · 10 min read

← Back to Blog

Running burns calories and punishes knees. Walking is kind to knees and burns few calories. There is a middle path that has quietly become one of the most practical fat-loss tools available, and it comes from military training: rucking — walking briskly with a weighted backpack. It raises the cost of every step without raising the impact of any of them, and it builds real-world strength at the same time.

Rucking is not a gimmick and it is not new. Soldiers have marched under load for centuries, and the reason it works for fat loss is simple physics: move more mass over the same distance and your body pays more energy to do it. The surprise is how large the difference is, and how little extra wear it puts on your joints compared with running the same calories off.

Why Rucking Beats Plain Walking for Fat Loss

A 80 kg person walking briskly burns roughly 250–300 calories an hour. Add a 15 kg pack and that figure climbs to roughly 400–500 calories an hour — a 50–70% increase for the same route, the same shoes, and the same time. The reason is that loaded walking raises oxygen consumption substantially while keeping one foot on the ground at all times, so impact forces stay near walking levels rather than the two to three times bodyweight that running delivers with every stride.

That low-impact profile is the real selling point for fat loss. The limiting factor in most fat-loss plans is not willpower, it is total weekly activity volume — and volume is limited by recovery. Running volume is capped by joint stress; rucking volume is capped mostly by time and general fatigue, which means most people can ruck several times a week, for months, without the shin splints, knee aches, and forced layoffs that kill running programs. The best fat-loss exercise is the one you can still do in week twelve, and rucking is unusually durable.

There is also a body-composition bonus walking does not offer: loaded walking builds the postural muscles of the back, the traps, the glutes, and the calves in a way unloaded walking never will. It will not replace strength training, but it does mean your cardio sessions contribute to the musculature that keeps you upright — a meaningful difference for people who sit all day.

The rule of thumb: start with a pack weighing about 10% of your bodyweight, walk briskly for 30–45 minutes, 2–3 times a week. Add 2–3 kg every couple of weeks as it gets easy, and cap most sessions around 20–25% of bodyweight unless you are training for something specific.

How Heavy, How Far, How Often

The beginner mistake is loading a pack like a soldier on day one. Start lighter than you think you need — 10% of bodyweight feels easy for the first ten minutes and honest by minute forty, which is exactly right. A 80 kg person starts around 8 kg: a few books, a water bladder, or a sandbag wrapped in a towel. Progress by adding small increments, not by doubling the load, and progress distance before load if you are choosing between the two.

Distance and pace matter more than heroic loads. A brisk pace — fast enough that talking takes effort but is still possible — over 3–5 km is a solid session. As fitness improves, extend toward 5–8 km before adding much weight. Two to three sessions a week is the sweet spot for fat loss alongside a normal strength program; daily rucking is possible at lighter loads, but heavy daily rucking competes with recovery from lifting, and lifting is what preserves muscle while you lose fat.

Terrain is a free intensity dial. Flat pavement is the baseline; gentle hills raise the cost substantially without any extra load, and trails add variety and balance work. Save steep, rough terrain for after you are comfortable with the load on flat ground — ankles under load on uneven ground are the most common rucking injury, and it is an avoidable one.

Form and Gear: The Details That Prevent Problems

Gear can be simple, but a few choices matter. A proper backpack with a hip belt beats a duffel or a fashion bag: the hip belt transfers much of the load to your legs, which is where it belongs, instead of hanging it all from your shoulders. Pack the weight high and close to your back, not loose at the bottom of the bag where it swings and pulls you backward. If you use plates or sandbags, wrap them so they cannot shift mid-walk.

Posture is the other half of injury prevention. Lean slightly forward from the ankles, not by hunching at the waist. Keep your chest open and your shoulders down and back — if your shoulders are creeping toward your ears, the load is too heavy or the straps need adjusting. Shorten your stride slightly compared with unloaded walking; long strides under load increase braking forces on the knees. And wear the shoes you would walk 5 km in, not fashion sneakers: support and a broken-in fit matter more under load than they do unloaded.

Two safety notes worth stating plainly. First, rucking is not ideal for everyone: people with existing back, hip, or knee problems, or with osteoporosis, should clear loaded walking with a clinician before starting. Second, heat matters more under load — a weighted walk on a hot day is substantially more demanding than the same walk unloaded, so carry water, start lighter in summer, and treat dizziness or sharp joint pain as a stop signal, not a challenge.

Rucking vs. Walking vs. Running

For fat loss, the comparison comes down to three numbers: calories per hour, joint cost, and sustainability. Plain walking is cheapest on joints and lowest on calories. Running is highest on calories but highest on joint cost, and its injury rate is what limits most people's weekly volume. Rucking sits in the sweet spot: roughly two-thirds of running's calorie burn at walking's joint cost, with a strength bonus neither of the other two offers. It is also the easiest of the three to do while listening to a podcast, thinking, or walking with someone — and enjoyment is a fat-loss variable too, because it determines whether you are still doing it in three months.

None of this means running is bad or walking is useless. It means that if running hurts you, or if walking alone is not moving the scale, adding a pack to the walk you already do is the smallest change with the largest payoff. The best program is the one built on a habit you already have, and most people already walk.

Common Rucking Mistakes

  • Starting too heavy. A 20 kg pack on day one is how backs get hurt and programs get abandoned. Start at 10% of bodyweight and earn the heavier loads.
  • Carrying the weight low and loose. Weight swinging at the bottom of a bag pulls your posture apart. Pack it high, tight, and close to your spine.
  • Skipping the hip belt. Shoulder-only carrying turns a leg workout into a neck and trap ache. Use the belt; that is what it is for.
  • Rucking through joint pain. Muscle fatigue is expected; sharp knee, ankle, or back pain is a signal to stop, unload, and reassess — not to push through.
  • Replacing lifting with rucking. Rucking preserves and builds some muscle, but it does not replace progressive strength training. In a fat-loss phase, lifting is what keeps the weight you lose from being muscle.

FAQ

Can I just use a regular backpack? Yes, to start — as long as it has a hip belt and sturdy straps. A dedicated ruck with a frame and weight plates is more comfortable at heavier loads, but a regular pack with books or a sandbag is fine for your first month.

How many calories does rucking actually burn? It depends on bodyweight, load, pace, and terrain, but a reasonable estimate is 1.5–2 times your unloaded walking burn at the same pace. A 80 kg person with 15 kg at a brisk pace burns roughly 400–500 calories an hour on flat ground, more on hills.

Will rucking make my legs bigger? Not dramatically — it builds endurance and some muscle in the calves, glutes, and postural muscles, but it is not a substitute for squats and deadlifts. Think of it as conditioning with a strength bonus, not a leg day.

Is rucking bad for your back? Done with reasonable loads, good posture, and a hip belt, rucking is generally back-friendly — the load is vertical and symmetrical, unlike many gym lifts. It becomes a back problem when loads jump too fast, posture collapses, or pre-existing issues are ignored. Start light and progress slowly.

Can I ruck every day? At light loads (10% of bodyweight or less), yes. At heavier loads, 2–4 sessions a week alongside strength training is a better balance. Daily heavy rucking competes with lifting recovery, and in a fat-loss phase, lifting recovery is the priority.

Bottom Line

Rucking is the rare fat-loss tool that is both more effective and more sustainable than the obvious alternatives: it burns roughly twice what plain walking burns, costs your joints far less than running, and builds the postural strength that desk life erodes. Start with 10% of your bodyweight, a pack with a hip belt, and a brisk 30–45 minute walk a few times a week, and progress slowly. The scale moves because the calories add up — and unlike a running program that ends in shin splints, a rucking habit is one you can still be doing a year from now, which is when fat loss actually counts.
