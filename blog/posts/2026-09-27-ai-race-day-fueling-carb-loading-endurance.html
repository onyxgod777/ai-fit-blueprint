AI Race-Day Fueling — Carb Loading, Sodium, and Caffeine That Actually Work

September 27, 2026 · 11 min read

← Back to Blog

Races are rarely lost in training. They are lost in the final week — by a carb load that was too small, a sodium plan that was never tested, a fluid target borrowed from a magazine, or a caffeine dose first tried at mile 18. You can be the fittest person on the start line and still walk the last 10K because your fueling fell apart.

The frustrating part is that race-day nutrition is genuinely solvable. The science is not mysterious: fill glycogen, replace what you sweat out, keep blood glucose steady during the event, and don't shock your gut with anything new. What has always been hard is doing the arithmetic for your body, your sweat rate, your gut, and your event duration at your intensity — plus the weather on the day.

That arithmetic is exactly what AI does well. Instead of a generic "eat pasta the night before" handout, an AI fueling plan is built from your own training data, your past race outcomes, and a forecast — and then adjusted in the days before the gun.

Why Most Race-Day Plans Fail

Four failure modes account for nearly every bonk, cramp, and porta-potty stop on course:

  • Under-fueling during the event. Most athletes take in far fewer carbohydrates per hour than they can absorb, and the deficit accumulates on a curve. At hour two you feel fine; at hour three and a half the lights go out.
  • Gut distress. Blood is shunted away from the digestive tract during hard effort, so anything unfamiliar, overly concentrated, or too high in fiber and fat sits there and causes cramps.
  • Sodium and fluid mismatch. Sweat sodium concentration varies several-fold between individuals and can change with heat acclimation, fitness, and diet. Copying someone else's salt plan is a coin flip.
  • Untested race-day experiments. New gels, new electrolytes, new caffeine timing — all first-tried when it matters most.

The common thread is that each failure is a personalization failure, not a willpower failure. Which is why the fix is a plan computed for you rather than a rule copied from someone else.

Key Insight: Race-day nutrition has a fixed structure — load glycogen before, deliver carbohydrate and fluid during, manage caffeine strategically — but every number inside that structure is individual. The plan only works when the numbers are yours.

Carb Loading, Done With Data

Carbohydrate loading works. It is one of the best-supported interventions in sports nutrition: raising muscle glycogen stores above normal before an endurance event measurably delays fatigue. The classic protocol is 8–12 g of carbohydrate per kilogram of body weight per day for 36–48 hours before the event, combined with a taper that drops training volume while keeping intensity sharp.

Where it goes wrong is in the numbers. A universal "eat more pasta" instruction ignores your body mass, your normal intake, and the fact that jumping from 3 g/kg to 10 g/kg overnight produces bloating, GI distress, and a huge scale jump that scares people who are tracking weight as part of a periodized carbohydrate strategy. The AI version calculates target grams per day from your body weight and current intake, spreads them across meals and snacks sized to your tolerance, and increases carbohydrate gradually over a few days instead of in one dramatic dinner.

The weight you "gain" is water, and it is the point

Every gram of glycogen is stored with roughly three grams of water. A successful carb load can add 1–2 kg to the scale. That is not fat, and it is not a mistake — it is fuel and the fluid bound to it, which is exactly what you want on board at the start line. Athletes who panic at that number and cut carbohydrates in the final 48 hours start the race with less fuel than they could have had.

Sweat Sodium and Fluid: Test, Don't Guess

Dehydration increases cardiovascular strain and pushes perceived effort up; over-drinking dilutes blood sodium and is far more dangerous. The safe window is narrower than most people think, and it is defined by your personal sweat rate and sweat sodium concentration.

Estimating sweat rate

Weigh yourself nude before and after a one-hour session at race intensity, note exactly how much you drank, and use the standard arithmetic: body-mass loss in grams plus fluid consumed in milliliters, divided by hours. A common result is 0.8–1.5 L per hour, but plenty of athletes sit outside that range in both directions. Repeat the test in heat, because your sweat rate climbs as conditions get hotter.

Why sodium matters more than the fluid volume

Sodium drives thirst and fluid retention. Take in plain water in large volumes without sodium and you dilute plasma sodium — the mechanism behind exercise-associated hyponatremia. AI fueling tools combine your measured sweat rate, the event's forecast temperature and humidity, and past intake data to produce an hourly fluid and sodium target rather than a single blanket number. If you have ever finished a long effort with white salt streaks on your kit or a pounding headache, your sodium concentration is probably on the high side and deserves a specific plan.

Carbohydrate Delivery During the Event

Once you are moving, the goal is a steady carbohydrate drip that keeps blood glucose available without overwhelming the gut. Modern evidence supports higher intake than the old 60 g/hour guideline — often 80–120 g/hour — but only when you use multiple transportable carbohydrate sources (glucose plus fructose) and train your gut to tolerate it over weeks, not days.

An AI plan sequences this for you: gels, chews, and drink mix scheduled at specific minutes rather than left to feel, with the mix rotated so you do not spend three hours consuming the same flavor. It also cross-references the course profile — carbohydrate intake is easier to tolerate on flats than on steep climbs, and the schedule can be shifted accordingly.

Key Insight: Gut training is a real adaptation. The stomach and intestine can be trained to absorb more carbohydrate per hour, but it takes four to eight weeks of practice. Race week is far too late to start — which is why the AI approach builds the intake schedule into your long training sessions first.

Caffeine: The Legal Ergogenic, Timed Properly

Caffeine reliably improves endurance performance at roughly 3–6 mg per kilogram of body weight, and its effect on perceived effort is often the bigger win. The strategic mistake is timing. A single large dose an hour before the gun peaks early and leaves you flat when the race gets hard.

Many athletes are better served by a modest pre-race dose plus smaller top-ups later in the event, including in the final third when perceived effort spikes and decision-making degrades. AI scheduling treats caffeine as a curve to manage across the event rather than a switch to flip once — and it accounts for your habitual intake, since regular heavy coffee drinkers get a blunted response and may want to taper caffeine intake beforehand.

Pacing Is a Fueling Decision

Intensity determines fuel use. Start too fast and you oxidize carbohydrate far above the rate you can replace it, emptying glycogen early and guaranteeing a late-race collapse. Start controlled and you burn a larger fraction of fat, spare glycogen, and still have carbohydrate available when the pace rises.

This is why pacing and fueling should be planned together. If your lactate threshold and VO2 max data say a target pace or power is sustainable, the fueling plan can be matched to the actual carbohydrate oxidation rate at that intensity. If the plan and the pacing disagree, one of them is wrong — and AI can flag that mismatch before race day instead of at kilometer 30.

A Practical Race Week Timeline

When What to do
7–4 days out Confirm the fueling plan; finish gut training; verify every product you will use on course
3–2 days out Begin carb load at target g/kg; reduce training volume; keep fiber and fat moderate to limit GI load
Day before Familiar, low-fiber meals on your normal schedule; hydrate with sodium; prepare bottles and gels; early night
3–4 hours pre-race Carbohydrate-rich, low-fiber, low-fat meal of roughly 1–3 g/kg; 500 ml fluid with sodium
60–30 min pre-race Pre-race caffeine dose if using one; small carbohydrate top-up; start sipping to thirst
During the event Carbohydrate and fluid on the scheduled clock, not on feel; sodium per your measured plan; caffeine top-ups in the back half
First 2 hours after Fluid with sodium plus carbohydrate and protein to start repletion — see post-workout nutrient timing

What AI Actually Changes

None of the underlying physiology is new. What AI changes is the arithmetic and the feedback loop:

  1. It computes your numbers. Carbohydrate grams per day, grams per hour, fluid liters per hour, sodium milligrams per hour, caffeine milligrams — all derived from your body mass, measured sweat data, and event duration rather than generic tables.
  2. It adjusts for conditions. Forecast heat and humidity shift fluid and sodium targets automatically, and altitude changes fluid losses and perceived effort.
  3. It learns from your races. If a previous event ended with GI distress or a late bonk, the plan for the next one changes rather than repeating the same mistake.
  4. It keeps fueling consistent with body composition goals. For athletes tracking leanness alongside performance, the plan integrates with a recomposition strategy so the carb load and the deficit do not fight each other in the final week.

The Bottom Line

Race day rewards preparation and punishes improvisation. Load glycogen properly with 8–12 g per kilogram for the final 36–48 hours, measure your sweat rate and sodium needs instead of guessing, take carbohydrate on a schedule rather than on feel, time caffeine like the performance tool it is, and pace in a way your fueling plan can actually support.

The difference between finishing strong and hanging on is almost never fitness. It is whether the numbers behind your plan were yours — computed from your body, your sweat, your gut, and the conditions on the day. That is the part AI handles better than any generic template ever could.
