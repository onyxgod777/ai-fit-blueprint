Deadlift Form for Beginners: Build Full-Body Strength Without Wrecking Your Back

November 7, 2026 · 10 min read

The deadlift is the most honest lift in the gym. It trains more muscle in a single movement than anything else you can do with a barbell — legs, glutes, back, grip, and core, all at once — and it has a direct carryover to real life, because picking heavy things off the floor is something bodies are built to do. It also has a reputation that scares beginners away, and that reputation is half-earned: done wrong, it is the lift most likely to leave your lower back aching for a week. Done right, it is one of the safest and most productive exercises there is. The difference is not strength or courage. It is setup, bracing, and patience.

The good news is that good deadlift form is a short checklist, not a talent. Feet under the bar, shins close, hips back, chest up, lats tight, push the floor away — and then stand up the same way every rep. Most of what goes wrong comes from rushing the setup or adding weight before the pattern is automatic. Below: the setup step by step, the brace that protects your spine, the pull itself, the mistakes that cause nearly all deadlift injuries, and the beginner program that builds the lift without breaking you.

The Setup: Where Good Reps Are Made

Stand with the bar over the middle of your feet — not your toes, not your shins, the middle of the whole foot, roughly where your shoelaces tie. Your shins should be about an inch from the bar before you bend down. Feet hip-width apart, toes pointed out slightly, weight balanced across the whole foot. This position matters more than anything else in the lift: if the bar starts too far forward, it will swing away from you on the way up and load your lower back; if it starts too close, your knees will shove it forward. Middle of the foot is the anchor for everything that follows.

Bend at the hips first, then the knees, and take your grip just outside your legs — hands about shoulder-width apart, arms hanging straight down like hooks. A double-overhand grip is the right start for a beginner: both palms facing you, which keeps the shoulders even and builds grip strength. Mixed grip (one palm in, one out) lets you hold more weight, but it twists the torso slightly and is better saved for heavier sets later. Then pull your chest up and your hips down until your shins touch the bar and your back is flat — not arched, not rounded, flat, with your shoulder blades sitting directly over the bar. That is the start position. If your hips are too high, you are stiff-legging it; if they are too low, you are squatting the bar. The right spot feels like a coiled spring: hamstrings loaded, lats engaged, weight on the mid-foot.

The Brace: Your Built-In Weight Belt

Before the bar moves, brace. Take a breath into your belly — not your chest — and push your abs out against an imaginary belt, as if someone were about to poke you in the stomach. That intra-abdominal pressure is what stiffens your spine; it is the single most protective thing you can do in a deadlift, and it matters more than any belt. A belt helps, but it is a tool that amplifies a brace you already have, not a replacement for one. Learn to brace without a belt first, and the belt will add to it later.

At the same time, squeeze your armpits — think about bending the bar around your shins or squeezing an orange under each arm. That cue fires the lats, the big back muscles that keep the bar glued to your legs during the pull. A deadlift that drifts away from the body is a deadlift that loads the lower back; lats tight, bar close, is the fix. The whole setup should take three to five seconds, not thirty. Breathe, brace, squeeze, pull.

The Pull: Push the Floor Away

Think of the deadlift as a push, not a pull. Press the floor away with your legs and let the bar rise as your hips and knees extend together. The bar should travel in a straight vertical line, dragging lightly up your shins and thighs — if your shins are not lightly scraped after a session, the bar drifted forward. Keep your chest up and your back flat through the whole lift; the moment your hips shoot up before your shoulders, your back is doing the work your legs should be doing, and that is where strains happen.

Lockout is simple: stand tall, hips fully extended, glutes squeezed, shoulders back — but do not lean back past vertical. Hyperextending at the top loads the lumbar spine for no benefit. Hold the lockout for a beat, then lower the bar by pushing your hips back first and bending the knees once the bar passes them, reversing the path it came up. Lowering under control matters: dropping the bar from the top teaches you nothing, and a controlled descent builds the hamstrings and the eccentric strength that protects you next time. Touch the floor, reset your breath and brace, and pull the next rep from a dead stop — that is why it is called a deadlift, and touch-and-go reps that bounce off the floor are a different, sloppier exercise.

The Mistakes That Cause Nearly All Deadlift Injuries

Four errors account for almost every deadlift problem a beginner will meet. First, rounding the lower back under load — usually from starting with the hips too high or the bar too far forward, or from simply adding weight faster than the pattern can handle. A rounded back is not automatically an injury, but it is the position where the spine is least protected, and it is the mistake to fix first. Second, yanking the bar off the floor: a violent jerk breaks your brace before the lift starts. Pull the slack out of the bar first — a gentle tug until you feel the weight — then push.

Third, letting the bar drift forward, which happens when the lats are loose or the setup started with the bar over the toes. The fix is the lat squeeze and the mid-foot start position. Fourth, adding weight before the form is automatic. The deadlift rewards patience more than any other lift: if you cannot do five clean reps at a weight, you have no business adding more, and a month of light, perfect reps builds more strength than a week of ugly heavy ones. Ego is the most common deadlift injury mechanism there is.

Programming: A Beginner Plan That Works

For a beginner, the deadlift belongs once a week, for three to five sets of five reps, after a warm-up of lighter sets — the classic 5-rep scheme that builds strength without accumulating fatigue. Start with a weight you can lift with perfect form for all five reps of all five sets, and add 2.5 to 5 kg a week. That sounds slow; it is not — it compounds to 60 to 120 kg of added strength in a year if you do not stall, and the slow progression is what keeps the form clean while the numbers climb.

Two variations are worth knowing. The trap-bar deadlift — lifting inside a hexagonal bar with handles at your sides — is the friendlier starting point for many beginners: the weight stays centered under you, the back stays more upright, and the grip is neutral, which makes the whole lift easier to learn. If your gym has one, starting there and moving to the straight bar after a few months is a sensible path, not a compromise. The Romanian deadlift, where the bar starts at the hips and lowers to mid-shin without touching the floor, is the best accessory for hamstring strength and the hip-hinge pattern — add three sets of eight after your main work once the basic pull is comfortable.

Recovery matters more with deadlifts than with most lifts, because the whole posterior chain is involved: one heavy session a week is plenty for a beginner, and two days of rest before heavy squats keeps the lower back fresh. Sleep and protein do the usual work — the deadlift does not change the recovery rules, it just punishes you faster for breaking them.

Who Should Be Careful

Most people can deadlift, but a few should take care. Anyone with a current disc issue, a history of sciatica, or a recent lower-back injury should get clearance from a clinician and start with a trap bar or a raised bar (blocks under the plates shorten the range of motion) before attempting a full pull from the floor. People with limited hip mobility often find their back rounds at the bottom no matter what they do — for them, the fix is hip-mobility work and a raised starting position, not forcing the range. And anyone who cannot yet hinge at the hips without rounding the back — the basic hip-hinge pattern — should practice it unloaded first: a broomstick along the spine, touching head, upper back, and tailbone through the whole hinge, is the drill that teaches the pattern in ten minutes.

FAQ

Is the deadlift bad for your back? No — a poorly performed deadlift is bad for your back. Done with a flat back, a proper brace, and sensible progression, deadlifting strengthens the muscles that protect the spine, and lifters who deadlift regularly tend to have stronger, more resilient backs than people who avoid the lift entirely.

How much weight should a beginner start with? The empty bar, or a light loaded bar you can lift for five perfect reps without your form breaking down — often 40 to 60 kg for most adults. The right starting weight is the one that lets you practice the pattern, not the one that impresses anyone.

Should I wear a belt? Not at first. Learn to brace without one, then add a belt once you are lifting weights where the brace alone feels insufficient — usually after a few months of consistent training. A belt amplifies a brace; it does not replace one.

What if my grip gives out before my legs do? That is normal for beginners, and it fixes itself: grip strength adapts quickly to deadlifting. Use chalk, keep the double-overhand grip as long as you can, and only switch to mixed grip or straps on your heaviest sets when the grip is genuinely the limiting factor.

Conventional or sumo — which is better for a beginner? Conventional is the simpler starting point: feet narrower, hands outside the knees, and a more natural hinge. Sumo (wide stance, hands inside the knees) suits some body types better and shortens the range of motion, but it is worth learning conventional first and trying sumo later if your proportions favor it.

Bottom Line

The deadlift is the highest-return lift a beginner can learn: one movement that trains the whole posterior chain, builds grip and core strength, and carries over to everything else you do in the gym and in life. The price of admission is a patient setup — bar over mid-foot, chest up, brace hard, lats tight — and a progression slow enough that your form stays clean as the weight climbs. Start light, pull the slack out, push the floor away, and add a little each week. Six months from now the weight on the bar will look nothing like today, and your back will be stronger for it, not worse.
