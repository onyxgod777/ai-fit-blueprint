Dips for Beginners: Form, Chest and Triceps Development, and How to Progress Safely

November 15, 2026 · 10 min read

Dips are the lift beginners skip and experienced lifters respect. They need no equipment beyond two parallel bars, they build the chest, triceps, and front shoulders with your own bodyweight, and they expose weak links faster than almost any other upper-body movement — if your shoulders are not ready, or your form is sloppy, dips will tell you immediately. That honesty is exactly why they belong in a beginner's program, done with the right progression and the right respect for the shoulder joint.

Below: what a good dip looks like, the three mistakes that hurt shoulders, a step-by-step progression from assisted to weighted, how to program dips so they keep building strength, the variations that shift the emphasis, and the shoulder-care work that keeps the lift sustainable.

What a Good Dip Looks Like

Grip the bars with your palms facing inward, arms straight, shoulders pulled down away from your ears, and your body held tall. Cross your ankles behind you and bend your knees slightly so your feet stay clear of the floor. Lower yourself by bending your elbows, letting them track back behind you rather than flaring out to the sides, and lean your torso slightly forward — about fifteen to twenty degrees — if you want the chest to do more of the work. Go down until your upper arms are roughly parallel to the floor, pause briefly without bouncing, and press back up until your arms are straight, without locking your elbows hard at the top. Breathe in on the way down, breathe out on the way up, and keep the movement smooth from start to finish.

Two details separate a real dip from a shoulder grinder. The first is shoulder position: your shoulders must stay down and back, not shrugged up toward your ears. The moment the shoulders creep up, the joint loses its stable position and the front of the shoulder takes load it is not built for. The second is depth: parallel is deep enough for almost everyone. Going lower feels like more work, and it is — but the extra range loads the front of the shoulder capsule in its most stretched position, and that is where dips injure people. Depth below parallel is an advanced option for people with excellent shoulder mobility, not a beginner target.

The Three Mistakes That Hurt Shoulders

The first mistake is flared elbows. When the elbows point out to the sides, the shoulder rotates internally under load, and the front of the joint gets pinched. Keep the elbows pointing back, close to the body, and think of pressing the bars down rather than pushing yourself up. A slight flare is natural, but elbows at ninety degrees to the torso is a shoulder problem waiting to happen.

The second mistake is bouncing out of the bottom. The stretch at the bottom of a dip is real, and it is tempting to use it like a spring. But the bounce loads the shoulder and the chest tendons suddenly, at the most vulnerable point in the range, and it is the most common way beginners strain something on dips. Pause for a beat at the bottom. If you cannot press out of a paused dip, the set is over — that is information, not failure.

The third mistake is doing dips before the shoulders are ready. Dips load the shoulder in a stretched, extended position, and they demand more from the joint than push-ups do. If you cannot do ten clean push-ups, or if you have any history of shoulder pain, start with the assisted progressions below rather than jumping straight to full bodyweight dips. A shoulder that hurts during dips is not a shoulder that needs to be pushed through — it is a shoulder that needs a regression and a bit of patience.

The Progression: From Assisted to Weighted

Start with bench dips. Sit on the edge of a bench with your hands beside your hips, feet on the floor, and lower your body by bending your elbows, keeping your back close to the bench. This is the easiest version, and it builds the basic pressing pattern without the full bodyweight load. When you can do three sets of fifteen clean bench dips, you are ready for the next step.

Next, use an assisted dip machine or a resistance band looped over the bars. The machine lets you dial in exactly how much help you get; the band is cheaper and available anywhere, though the help it gives varies through the range — most at the bottom, least at the top, which is actually convenient. Work with assistance until you can do three sets of eight to ten reps with control, then reduce the help. When you can do five unassisted dips with good form, drop the assistance entirely.

Unassisted dips are the main event. Build from sets of five toward sets of ten to twelve, and only add weight once you can do three sets of twelve with a pause at the bottom. Add weight with a dip belt and plates, in small increments — two and a half kilos at a time is plenty. Weighted dips are a serious strength builder, but they are also where the shoulder risk goes up, so never add weight to a dip that already hurts, and never chase a heavy single on this lift.

Chest or Triceps? It Depends on Your Lean

The same movement trains different muscles depending on your torso angle. Lean forward with your elbows slightly out and your feet behind you, and the chest takes the bigger share — this is the version bodybuilders use to build the lower chest. Stay upright with your elbows tucked tight and your body vertical, and the triceps take over — this is the version for arm development. Both are valid, and most people should train the middle ground: a slight forward lean, elbows close, and a full range to parallel.

Neither version is better in absolute terms; the better question is what you need. If your bench press stalls at the lockout, upright triceps dips will help. If your chest development lags, the forward-leaning version is the tool. Train both angles across the week rather than picking one forever, and keep the shoulder rules — elbows back, shoulders down, pause at the bottom — identical in both.

How to Program Dips

For beginners, two sessions a week is plenty. Do three sets after your main pressing work, or on a separate upper-body day, and keep two or three reps in reserve — dips to failure are a shoulder risk, not a badge of honor. A good starting template is three sets of five to eight reps, adding a rep per week until you reach twelve, then adding a set, then adding weight. That slow progression is the whole secret: dips reward patience and punish ego.

Pair dips with a pulling movement in the same session. A set of dips followed by a set of rows or pull-ups balances the shoulder and keeps the upper back strong enough to stabilize the pressing. The classic pairing is dips and pull-ups on the same day, alternating sets, and it is a classic for a reason — the two movements cover the entire upper body with almost no equipment.

Recovery matters more on dips than on most lifts, because the shoulder is involved so deeply. Give yourself at least forty-eight hours between heavy dip sessions, and if your shoulders feel achy the day after, push the next session back a day. Soreness in the chest and triceps is normal; aching in the front of the shoulder is a signal to back off, check your form, and possibly regress to an easier variation for a week.

Variations Worth Knowing

Ring dips are the advanced version: the rings move freely, which demands far more stability and works the shoulders and core harder. Do not attempt them until strict bar dips are easy. Straight-bar dips, done on a single bar with the hands in front, are a calisthenics staple that shifts the load and builds a different kind of control. Korean dips, where the bar is behind you, are an advanced skill with a much higher shoulder risk — they look impressive and they are, but they are not a beginner exercise and not necessary for building strength.

The variation most beginners should actually use is the simplest one: tempo dips. Lower for three seconds, pause for one, press up in one. The slow descent builds the control and the tendon strength that makes the fast version safe, and it makes light dips feel hard enough to be useful. If your dips feel easy, slow them down before you add weight.

Shoulder Care for Dip Training

Two habits keep dips sustainable. The first is a proper warm-up: a few minutes of arm circles, band pull-aparts, and scapular push-ups before your first set, and a set or two of easy bench dips or assisted dips as a ramp-up. Cold shoulders and dips are a bad combination. The second is pulling work — rows, pull-ups, face pulls — done at least as often as your pressing. The shoulders stay healthy when the back is as strong as the front, and dips are a front-dominant lift that needs that balance.

If dips hurt, stop and diagnose rather than push through. Pain in the front of the shoulder usually means the elbows are flaring, the depth is too deep, or the volume is too high. Pain in the sternum or the chest tendon usually means the weight or the volume jumped too fast. In either case, the fix is the same: regress the variation, reduce the depth to parallel, cut the volume in half for two weeks, and rebuild. Shoulders forgive a careful lifter and punish a stubborn one.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are dips bad for your shoulders? No — done with elbows tucked, shoulders down, and depth to parallel, dips are a safe and effective lift. Shoulders get hurt when dips are done with flared elbows, excessive depth, bouncing, or too much weight too soon. Fix the form and the progression, and the lift is safe.

Can I do dips if I cannot do a single one yet? Yes — start with bench dips, then use an assisted machine or a resistance band. Most people can work from bench dips to a first full dip in four to eight weeks of consistent practice, two sessions a week.

How deep should I go on dips? Until your upper arms are parallel to the floor. Going deeper loads the front of the shoulder in its most stretched position, and for most people that extra range is not worth the risk. Parallel is the target; deeper is an advanced option, not a requirement.

Are dips better than push-ups? They are different. Dips load the chest and triceps with your full bodyweight and allow weight to be added, which makes them better for building maximal strength. Push-ups are easier on the shoulders and easier to scale. Both belong in a program, and neither replaces the other.

How often should I do dips? Twice a week is a good starting point, with at least forty-eight hours between sessions. Three sessions is fine once you are adapted, but dips are demanding on the shoulders, so more is not automatically better.

Bottom Line

Dips are one of the best upper-body lifts you can do with no equipment beyond two bars, and one of the easiest to do badly. Keep your elbows back and your shoulders down, lower to parallel and pause instead of bouncing, and earn your way from bench dips to assisted dips to full bodyweight reps before you think about adding weight. Lean forward for chest, stay upright for triceps, and balance every set of pressing with a set of pulling. Be patient with the progression — dips reward slow, steady work and punish shortcuts, and the lifters who respect the shoulder rules are the ones still doing dips years later. A set of ten clean, paused, bodyweight dips is a genuine strength milestone, and a set of weighted dips after that is one of the most satisfying lifts in the gym.
